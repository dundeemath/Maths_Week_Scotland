Fractal

A fractal is a structure that possessed the property of self-similarity. Specifically, it refers to a structure where the exact same shape repeatedly appears when the entire figure is broken down into several smaller parts.

While fractal patterns and shapes appear complex at first glance, they are actually the result of repeating simple rules and operations.

While fractals are a concept rooted in geometry, the fractal approach can also be applied to analyse various phenomena, such as stock prices, currency fluctuations, and social structures.

Many examples of fractals can also be observed in the natural world. These include a wide variety of examples, such as snowflakes, seashells (like turban shells and nautiluses), the shapes of cumulonimbus clouds, and the circulatory system in the human body (including the heart, blood vessels, and lymphatic vessels).

Build a fractal fern yourself

Below, build a fractal fern yourself, using exactly the same idea as the real fern pictured above: just four simple rules, each applied at random with its own fixed probability, plotting one point at a time. No fern shape is ever drawn directly – it emerges purely from repeating those four rules tens of thousands of times.

Everything below is built from one idea, introduced in three steps: start with a single point and a single rule, then pick between a few different shrink-towards-a-corner rules at random, step by step, then use that same idea with Barnsley’s own four rules to build a fern.

Turn on “By generating rule” to see the self-similarity directly: colour each point by which of the four rules drew it, and the largest green portion – on its own – already looks like a complete, smaller fern. That is exactly what it means to break a structure down into smaller parts that are the same shape as the whole.
