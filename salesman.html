The Travelling Salesman problem

Combinatorics
Algorithms
Discrete maths
Optimisation

Between the Fibonacci stop and this one, the trail passes four other stops – Phyllotaxis, Analemma, Königsberg and Curvature. Suppose you wanted to visit each of the stops once whilst minimising the distance that you travel.

This is the Travelling Salesman Problem (TSP) – one of the most famous problems in mathematics and computer science. It sounds simple: just try every order of stops and keep the shortest. With 5 stops there are only 12 distinct routes to check. With 10 stops there are already 181,440. With 20 stops there are over 60 quadrillion – so many that a computer checking a billion routes every single second would still need about two years to get through them all. Trying every option, which works fine for a handful of stops, becomes hopeless almost immediately – and no computer, however fast, escapes that by brute force alone.

Real versions of this problem sit behind delivery route planning, circuit board drilling, and DNA sequencing.

The app below shows six of the trail stops: φ Fibonacci (fixed as your start) and μ Travelling Salesman (fixed as your end), plus the four stops in between – Phyllotaxis, Analemma, Königsberg and Curvature. Tap those four, in whichever order you think gives the shortest route from start to end, then use the toggle in the sidebar to compare:

  • Your route – the order you tapped the four middle stops in.
  • Optimal route – the true shortest order, found by checking all 24 possibilities exactly.

With only four stops to order there are 24 possible routes in total – small enough to check every single one, which is exactly what the app does for the optimal route. Switch between the two and see how close your guess got.
