Why doesn’t the Earth fall onto the Sun?

Simulating a planet orbiting the sun?

The app below demonstrates why the earth does not fall into the sun. Change the initial speed and direction of the orbiting object. Can you find conditions for which the planet crashes into the sun rather than stay in an elliptical orbit?

To understand how the simulations work we need to go back to the discoveries of Galileo, Kepler and Newton.

1. Galileo: evidence that not everything orbits the Earth

In 1610, Galileo discovered four moons orbiting Jupiter. Press play below and watch Jupiter’s four largest moons complete their real orbital periods. Note that the inner-most moons orbit faster than the outer-most ones.

2. Kepler: ellipses, equal areas, and a hidden pattern

Johannes Kepler found that planets in the solar system do not move in perfect circles, nor at constant speed. He distilled the pattern into three laws: - orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one focus (not the centre); - a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times, moving faster when close to the Sun and slower when far away; - the square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the orbit’s size

Below you can explore all three below with Mercury, Earth, Mars and Jupiter’s real orbital data. Against note that inner-most planets orbit faster than outer-most ones.

3. Newton: why doesn’t it just fall in?

Kepler described what orbits look like; Newton explained why. His law of universal gravitation says the Sun pulls every planet straight towards itself, with a force that falls off as the square of the distance – and nothing more. So why doesn’t that pull simply drag every planet straight into the Sun? The answer is sideways motion: a planet is always “falling” towards the Sun, but it is also always moving sideways fast enough to keep missing it. In the app at the top of the page, you control a planet’s launch speed and direction directly. Aim it steeply inward without enough sideways speed, and it crashes. Give it enough sideways speed, and the very same gravity that would have pulled it in instead bends its path into a closed ellipse – or, with enough speed, flings it away for good. The path is coloured by the planet’s instantaneous speed – watch it turn bright near the Sun and dark on the far side of an elliptical orbit, direct visual evidence of Kepler’s second law: the planet moves fastest exactly where it’s closest to the Sun.

4. Bonus – Einstein: the ellipse itself slowly turns

Newton’s theory predicts a perfectly closed ellipse, retraced forever. For most planets that is an excellent match to reality – but not quite perfect. Mercury’s orbit, observed over centuries, precesses: its ellipse itself slowly rotates, by about 43 arcseconds per century beyond what Newtonian gravity (accounting for the pull of the other planets) predicts. This tiny leftover puzzled astronomers for decades, until Einstein’s general relativity explained it as a consequence of gravity being, at heart, curved spacetime rather than a force acting instantly at a distance. The demo below adds a simplified correction to gravity with the right qualitative shape (it is not the exact general-relativistic formula) and turns the dial up far beyond Mercury’s real, tiny effect, so the precession becomes visible over a handful of orbits instead of centuries.

The Analemma is an unequal figure-of-eight shape, created by tracking the sun’s position in the sky at the same time each day over a period of one year. The Analemma traces that figure-of-eight because the Earth’s orbit isn’t a perfect circle travelled at constant speed. Instead, it’s an ellipse, and Earth moves faster when it’s closer to the Sun. Working out exactly why orbits look like this took the better part of three centuries. Galileo discovered that not everything orbits the Earth; Kepler, worked out the precise shape of orbit based on observations; Newton explained why from a single law of gravity; and Einstein’s’ general relativity is needed to explain a tiny mismatch between Newton’s theory and Mercury’s orbit.
